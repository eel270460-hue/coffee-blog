There is nothing quite as frustrating as watching a brew that started beautifully suddenly grind to a halt on the 4th or 5th pour. 

You set up your recipe, the bloom looked lush, and the first couple of pulses drained right on schedule. But by the time you pour your final center stream, the slurry barely moves. The brew time stretches past four minutes, leaving you with a bitter, astringent, and over-extracted cup.

If you enjoy multi-pour techniques like the **4:6 method** or brew high-clarity light roasts, you have almost certainly hit this wall. 

Here is what is actually happening inside your filter cone during those final stages—and four quick adjustments to keep your drawdown smooth and fast.

---

## The Real Culprit: Fines Migration and Slurry Agitation

When coffee bed drawdown slows down near the end, it is rarely because your overall grind size is "too fine." The real culprit is **fines migration**.

Every coffee grinder, no matter how high-end or unimodal, produces a spectrum of particle sizes. The microscopic particles—known as **fines**—behave very differently from the main particles. 

During the initial pours, these fines float suspended in the water bed. But as you continue to pour water and agitate the slurry:

1. **Agitation pushes fines downward:** Heavy turbulence from high pours forces microscopic particles toward the bottom of the coffee bed.
2. **Pores get blinded:** The fines settle directly into the micro-cavities of your paper filter.
3. **The filter chokes:** Once enough paper pores are physically blocked by fines, water flow drops exponentially, regardless of how much liquid is left in the cone.

---

## 4 Ways to Prevent Final-Stage Clogging

### 1. Lower Your Pouring Height on Later Pulses
The force of your water stream is the primary cause of fines migration. High pours create deep turbulence, stirring up the coffee bed and washing fines straight into the filter wall.

* **First 1–2 pours:** Pour from a medium height to build temperature and agitate for even extraction.
* **Final pours:** Lower your kettle spout as close to the liquid surface as possible. Use a slow, gentle spiral or center pour to minimize bed disruption.

---

### 2. Settle the Slurry Less (Ditch the Excessive Swirling)
Swirling the dripper after your last pour is a popular technique to flatten the coffee bed, but it comes with a trade-off. Centrifugal force drives the smallest particles straight toward the paper filter.

> **Rule of thumb:** If your brews regularly clog, limit yourself to one very gentle swirl during the bloom—and skip swirling on the final pours entirely. Let gravity do the work.

---

### 3. Adjust Your Pour Timing (The Slurry Height Strategy)
If you wait until the coffee bed is completely dry before starting your next pour, the coffee bed collapses. When fresh water hits a collapsed bed, it violently disturbs the settled fines.

Instead, start your next pour when the water level drops to **about 1 cm above the coffee bed**. Maintaining a steady head of water buffers the impact of fresh stream and keeps the bed structure stable.

---

### 4. Re-Evaluate Your Grinder's Fine Production

If you have tried adjusting your pouring height and agitation but still experience drawdown stalls, your grinder may be generating an excessive amount of fines. 

Switching to a hand grinder designed specifically for light-roast pour-overs—featuring high-clarity burrs—makes an immediate difference.

## Quick Troubleshooting Checklist

Next time you brew, try this quick combination:

* [ ] Keep the kettle spout low to the water line on pour 4 and 5.
* [ ] Avoid swirling the dripper near the end of the brew.
* [ ] Start your next pulse while there is still a thin layer of liquid above the bed.
* [ ] Consider fast-flowing filter papers (like Cafec Abaca or Sibarist) if using dense light roasts.

By controlling agitation rather than simply coarsening your grind, you can keep your drawdown fast while preserving all the delicate sweetness and brightness in your cup.
