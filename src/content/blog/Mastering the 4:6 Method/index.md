When Tetsu Kasuya won the 2016 World Brewers Cup with his 4:6 method, it completely flipped the specialty coffee script. Before Kasuya, standard pour-over logic dictated continuous pouring, maintaining a constant water height, and avoiding a dry coffee bed at all costs to prevent temperature drops and channel formation.

Kasuya did the exact opposite: coarse grinding, pulse pouring, and letting the water drain completely between pours. 

If you’ve ever brewed a high-end washed Ethiopian or a delicate floral Geisha using traditional continuous pour methods, you might have noticed how easy it is to blur the top-note flavors into a muddy, heavy-bodied cup. That is where the 4:6 method shines. By breaking extraction into distinct structural phases, you gain direct control over the balance between sweetness, acidity, and strength—delivering the clean flavor separation that light roast enthusiasts crave.

Here is how to master the 4:6 method, specifically tuned for light roasts and high-clarity setups.

---

## The Core Science: Deconstructing the 4:6 Ratio

The beauty of the 4:6 method lies in its mathematical simplicity. You split your total brew water into two main parts:

1. **The First 40% (Acidity vs. Sweetness Balance):** Divided into two initial pours. This portion extracts the highest concentration of organic acids, sugars, and fast-dissolving volatile aromatics. 
2. **The Remaining 60% (Strength and Body):** Divided into two, three, or four equal pours. This portion controls concentration ($TDS$) and heavy compounds.

$$EY = \frac{TDS \times m_{\text{liquid}}}{m_{\text{dose}}}$$

While traditional brewing links extraction yield ($EY$) and concentration ($TDS$) tightly together, pulse pouring gives you independent levers to pull.
### Adjusting the First 40%
For a standard 20g dose to 300g water ratio (1:15), your first 40% equates to **120g of water**. You split this into two pours:

* **Balanced Cup:** 60g + 60g
* **Sweeter Cup (Less Perceived Acidity):** 50g + 70g (A smaller first pour reduces early acid extraction).
* **Brighter Cup (Higher Acidity):** 70g + 50g (A larger first pour draws out more bright, soluble fruit acids early on).

## The Light-Roast Mod: Why Kasuya’s Original Recipe Needs Tweaking

If you follow Kasuya’s original recipe word-for-word on a light roast brewed with modern unimodal hand grinders (like the **1Zpresso ZP6** or **Pietro**), you might end up with a cup that tastes sour, thin, or under-extracted. 

Kasuya originally designed this method using medium-to-dark roasts and traditional grinders that produced a lot of fines. For dense, light-roasted Scandinavian or ultra-light specialty beans, we need three critical modifications:

1. **Water Temperature:** Bump it up. Move from 88°C–92°C to **94°C–96°C** (201°F–205°F). Light roasts require high thermal energy to unlock complex sugars.
2. **Grind Size:** Instead of "very coarse" (French Press territory), aim for **medium-coarse**. On a 1Zpresso ZP6 Special, this sits around **4.5 to 5.0**. You want drawdown to be crisp without stalling.
3. **Agitation Control:** Keep your pour height low (around 3 to 5 cm above the bed). Heavy agitation drives fines down to the filter paper weave, causing late-stage clogging.

## Step-by-Step Brew Guide (The High-Clarity Light Roast Recipe)

### Equipment & Parameters
* **Coffee Dose:** 20g (Light Roast)
* **Water Volume:** 300g (1:15 ratio)
* **Water Temp:** 95°C (203°F)
* **Grinder Setting:** Medium-Coarse (ZP6 @ 4.8 / Comandante @ 26-28 clicks)
* **Dripper:** Hario V60 Plastic 02 with Cafec Abaca or Hario Dutch-made filters
### Brewing Execution

#### Step 1: Rinse & Preheat (0:00)
Rinse your paper filter with boiling water to flush out paper taste and thoroughly heat up the plastic V60. Dump the rinse water. Zero out your scale with 20g of coffee. Give the dripper a gentle tap to level the bed.

#### Step 2: The First Pour — The Anchor (0:00 - 0:45)
Start timer. Pour **60g** of water in slow, tight concentric circles from the center outward, avoiding the filter paper walls. Keep kettle spout low to limit agitation.

Let it drain. Unlike continuous brewing where you pour before the bed dries, **wait until the water drops almost completely through the bed** before the next pour. You should hear a soft whistling sound as air draws into the bed.

#### Step 3: The Second Pour — Balancing Sweetness (0:45 - 1:30)
At 0:45, pour another **60g** (total scale reading: **120g**). Spiral out and back in within 10 seconds, then let it drain. This completes the 40% phase. 

#### Step 4: The 60% Phase — Building Strength (1:30 - 3:00)
Pour three identical pulses of **60g** each at 1:30, 2:15, and 3:00:
* **1:30:** Pour to **180g**
* **2:15:** Pour to **240g**
* **3:00:** Pour to **300g**

> **Crucial Technique Note:** Do not swirl the dripper! Swirling during a pulse-pour setup forces micro-fines into the bottom tip of the filter cone, clogging the exit hole and causing severe astringency. Let gravity and light water flow do the work.

#### Step 5: Final Drawdown (~3:30 - 3:45)
After the last pour at 3:00, the water level should drain completely by **3:30 to 3:45**. Your coffee bed should look flat and uniform, without deep craters or thick muddy sludge on the sides.

## Dialing In: Troubleshooting Your Cup

If the flavor isn't hitting the mark, use this diagnostic cheat sheet to tweak your variables:

| Sensory Profile | Cause | The Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Astringent, dry finish (tannic/tealike)** | Heavy fine migration / over-agitation | Lower pouring height, grind 1-2 notches coarser, or avoid swirling. |
| **Sour, hollow, empty finish** | Under-extraction due to low thermal mass | Increase water temp to 96°C or grind slightly finer. |
| **Muddled flavor, heavy body, lack of clarity** | Too many pours in the 60% section | Combine the last three pours into two equal pours (90g + 90g). |
| **Drawdown takes longer than 4:15** | Filter clogging from fines | Upgrade to lower-fine filters (e.g., Cafec Abaca) or clean your grinder burrs. |

## Final Thoughts

The 4:6 method isn't just a rigid recipe—it's a modular framework. Once you master how the initial 40% dictates flavor character and the remaining 60% dictates cup strength, you can adapt it to any single-origin bean in your stash.

For high-clarity lovers, keeping agitation low, temperatures high, and grinds uniform turns this method into an absolute flavor amplifier. Give this recipe a spin on your next light roast washed bag, and taste the separation for yourself.
